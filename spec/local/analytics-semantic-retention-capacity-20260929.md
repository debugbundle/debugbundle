# Semantic raw-retention capacity check — 2026-09-29

Status: local measurement and capacity model. This is **not** a production-volume qualification or permission to enable V2 capture.

## Allowance and service budget

The Team analytics event allowance is 250,000 per capacity unit per organization billing month. Team includes 15 units and permits up to 99 additional purchased units. The table normalizes a month to 30 days only to compare steady rates; an actual billing period is server-owned and may have a different duration.

| Team units | Monthly event allowance | Normalized steady expiry per minute |
| ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 15 included | 3,750,000 | 86.8 |
| 15 + 10 purchased | 6,250,000 | 144.7 |
| 15 + 99 purchased | 28,500,000 | 659.7 |

The current semantic catch-up lease starts at most once a minute, processes at most ten 100-object batches and has a 30-second start deadline. Its absolute ceiling is 1,000 raw objects per minute, even with zero S3/DB latency. The installed Team ingestion limit is 10,000 events per minute **per token**; several tokens can admit more within the organization allowance. The monthly quota does not guarantee a smooth arrival rate.

At the 99-unit maximum, a one-hour 10,000-event/minute burst followed by 659.7/minute steady traffic would create about 540,000 due objects beyond the catch-up ceiling during its expiry hour. At the theoretical 1,000/minute ceiling, the net post-burst drain is only 340.3/minute, taking about 26.4 hours. Some raw objects would exceed the 24-hour cleanup-lag invariant even if every deletion succeeds. This is a deterministic capacity failure under allowed usage, separate from host speed. The six-hour installed V1 lane cannot be counted as a guaranteed semantic burst drain, since it has other retention work and its own bounded passes.

The token limit is not an organization-wide instantaneous limit. Multiple writer tokens can consume more of the same 28.5-million-event monthly allowance in a short interval, so this one-token burst is a **lower-bound failure example**, not the worst allowed arrival curve. Any qualifying design needs an explicit aggregate admission envelope or a measured cleanup capacity and fairness model for concurrent tokens and projects. The monthly allowance alone cannot bound per-minute expiry.

One project may have up to ten active analytics writers. At the Team per-token 10,000/minute limit, ten fully used server writers could consume the 28.5-million maximum purchased monthly event allowance in **285 minutes (4 h 45 min)** if all other admission conditions and transport capacity permit. The eventual expiry cliff would require **19,792 successful deletes/minute** to clear in 24 hours even without continuing traffic or failed calls. This is a theoretical allowed-arrival stress envelope, not observed ingestion throughput; multiple projects can also share one organization's allowance. At the 16-KiB per-event wire maximum, 28.5 million accepted records represent about **435 GiB of pre-gzip event bytes** before S3/object metadata and compression effects. Three such 30-day billing periods can leave up to **85.5 million transport receipts** within a 90-day replay horizon, with project-lifetime business dedupe keys adding separate growth. These counts need row/object storage and indexing qualification before activation.

The candidate server outbox route now uses the existing hosted per-credential limiter at the current tier's rate before staging a valid event batch; Red/Green HTTP tests cover denial, original indexes and missing rate-policy failure, and the PostgreSQL/LocalStack writer case claims once per request. This fixes a bypass in that candidate route but does **not** alter the 10,000/minute Team token limit, aggregate multi-writer arrival or the cleanup-capacity conclusion. The composed route remains disabled.

Retention changes create another independent burst. The current semantic cleanup computes eligibility from each receipt's acceptance time and the **current** `project_analytics_settings.raw_retention_days`. Reducing a project from a long period toward the one-day minimum can make most previously accepted objects due in one schedule, even if future V2 arrivals receive an organization-wide minute cap. Near the maximum purchased Team monthly allowance, an illustrative 28.5-million-object due backlog needs 28,500 minutes, or 19.8 days, at the theoretical 1,000/minute ceiling before new arrivals or failures. The exact reachable count depends on the prior setting, billing period and object age; the point is that aggregate ingress throttling alone cannot prove the 24-hour lag rule. A qualifying design must specify and test how current-setting reductions clear existing data promptly without weakening privacy, as well as live-arrival bursts.

For scale, clearing 28.5 million due objects in 24 hours requires **19,792 successful deletes/minute** before new expiry, retries and receipt-pruning work. At maximum purchased Team steady expiry, the net requirement becomes about **20,452/minute**. This is a lower bound for that illustrative backlog, not an endorsed scheduler setting: hosted object latency, failed S3 calls, database contention, multiple projects and a bounded operator safety margin increase required capacity. The current 1,000/minute absolute cap is about one twentieth of that lower bound. A bulk/parallel, safely fenced deletion design and representative load proof are needed before choosing a rollout limit; changing the batch constant alone would not establish safety.

## Disposable measurement

`make semantic-retention-capacity-check` creates an isolated PostgreSQL/Redis/LocalStack Compose project, seeds one Team project with 2,000 expired completed semantic receipts and 2,000 real protected S3 objects, runs two competing PostgreSQL read loops and two small update loops, and executes two actual semantic catch-up passes. It tears down its dedicated containers and volumes after the run. The source is `tests/integration/semantic-analytics-retention-capacity.integration.test.ts`. These passes are invoked back to back; 1,000/minute is the **schedule ceiling** because the job normally starts once a minute, not a throughput estimate extrapolated from the few-second local execution time.

The 2026-09-29 run in `/private/tmp/semantic-retention-capacity-1.log` passed: the first pass deleted 1,000 objects in 2,298 ms and left 1,000; the second deleted the remaining 1,000 in 2,060 ms. There were 868 competing PostgreSQL reads. Both passes reached the configured batch ceiling well before the 30-second deadline, so **the schedule cap**, rather than measured LocalStack latency, limited this small local run. The fixture is 2,000 objects in one project; it does not model millions of receipt/index rows, real hosted object-store latency, concurrent ingest/projection writes, mixed worker jobs, failed deletions at scale, multi-token bursts or worker restarts. The existing smaller integration suite covers one failed S3 object backing off five minutes and later expiry progressing.

A second run in `/private/tmp/semantic-retention-capacity-2.log` added real S3 absence assertions for the first and last seeded objects and derived allowance rates from `TIER_CAPABILITIES`. It passed with 1,000 deletions in 3,529 ms and 1,000 in 4,076 ms during 1,492 competing reads. The local timing varied while the fixed batch ceiling remained the limiting factor.

The combined final integration run in `/private/tmp/semantic-delivery-final-integration.log` passed the capacity check alongside 17 current-policy/HTTP tests: the two deletion passes took 3,539 ms and 2,906 ms during 1,268 competing reads. The first and last real S3 objects were absent afterward. This remains a local, small-backlog measurement, not a hosted throughput claim.

A subsequent isolated run with competing writes passed in `/private/tmp/semantic-retention-capacity-write-load.log`: 1,000 deletions in 3,466 ms and the remaining 1,000 in 3,055 ms, alongside 644 reads and 637 updates to separate worker-job rows. It confirmed the configured batch ceiling under modest database write contention. It does not model production write volume, row counts, shared worker saturation, or hosted object-store behavior.

An overlap regression first failed in `/private/tmp/semantic-retention-overlap-red.log`: two cleanup owners issued S3 deletes for the same receipt while the first call remained in flight. The receipt's existing five-minute retry timestamp now also acts as a per-object in-flight lease, set under the job/project/receipt lock before calling S3. A second owner skips the object until the lease expires; a crashed or failed call can retry after that bound. The four-file PostgreSQL/LocalStack suite passes 43 cases in `/private/tmp/semantic-retention-overlap-green.log`, including receipt replay, worker observation, the overlap test and the 2,000-object capacity exercise. With this extra row update, the two passes took 2,267 ms and 2,244 ms alongside 450 reads and 448 writes. This removes duplicate in-flight S3 calls but does **not** increase the 1,000/minute schedule ceiling or qualify parallel worker scaling.

A second Red regression in `/private/tmp/semantic-retention-lease-clock-red.log` showed that deriving this lease from the queued job's older scheduled time lets a later owner treat an in-flight delete as expired. The lease and retry now use PostgreSQL's execution clock; the scheduled time still controls the raw-retention cutoff. Retry tests advance the stored lease in disposable PostgreSQL rather than advancing only a simulated schedule. This protects a delayed job from immediate duplicate S3 deletion without changing V1 scheduling.

The four-file rerun passes all 43 cases in `/private/tmp/semantic-retention-lease-clock-green.log`. The capacity pass deleted 1,000 objects in 2,348 ms and the next 1,000 in 2,090 ms alongside 440 reads and 440 writes. These timings are disposable local evidence; the purchased-capacity burst failure and representative hosted qualification remain open.

The semantic-only cleanup runner reports aggregate `raw_deleted`, `receipts_pruned`, batch count, elapsed time and whether its batch or deadline bound was reached. The worker emits `semantic_raw_retention_progress` when it did work or has a remaining-work hint; it logs no event, project, credential or object identifiers. A full last batch is only a **hint** that more work may exist: the second 1,000-object pass can report the hint even when the database has zero remaining rows. The isolated rerun in `/private/tmp/semantic-retention-progress-capacity.log` passed with two ten-batch progress records, each reporting 1,000 raw deletions and no deadline hit; PostgreSQL verification found all 2,000 deleted. The focused retention/worker suite passes 52 tests in `/private/tmp/semantic-retention-progress-green.log`.

The runner now also aggregates `raw_delete_failures` and `oldest_selected_due_at`, the earliest due time among objects actually selected during its bounded passes. A failed S3 delete logs progress even if no object was removed. A focused Red assertion in `/private/tmp/semantic-retention-observability-red.log` preceded implementation; **52 retention/worker tests** and **14 PostgreSQL/LocalStack retention cases** pass in `/private/tmp/semantic-retention-observability-green-units.log` and `/private/tmp/semantic-retention-observability-integration.log`. The selected due time is **not** the oldest remaining overdue object or a per-project fairness measure; retry-deferred objects may be omitted. Oldest remaining age, per-project fairness and representative failure rate still require explicit bounded queries and production-like load proof.

The isolated capacity rerun after the additional due-time selection passed in `/private/tmp/semantic-retention-observability-capacity.log`: 1,000 deletes in 2,139 ms and 1,000 in 2,226 ms, with 434 competing reads and 434 updates. Both progress records reported zero S3 failures and selected due time `2026-09-26T12:00:00.000Z`; all 2,000 objects were removed. This small fixture still reaches the fixed schedule ceiling, and the new field does not prove the age of remaining work.

## Bounded multi-object deletion candidate

The semantic-only raw cleanup now uses S3 `DeleteObjects` in batches of at most 100 selected receipts when the adapter provides it. Every object is first placed in its existing durable five-minute deletion lease under the job → project → receipt lock order. The adapter requires an exact per-key success/failure partition from S3; a missing, duplicate or foreign response key fails the whole call closed. A partial S3 failure marks only confirmed successes deleted and leaves failed objects retryable. The legacy single-delete fallback and V1 raw-retention lane remain available and unchanged. A Red adapter test preceded the new method (`/private/tmp/semantic-bulk-s3-red.log`); **33 adapter unit tests** and **15 real PostgreSQL/LocalStack raw-retention cases** pass, including partial bulk success, failed-key retry and actual object absence (`/private/tmp/semantic-bulk-s3-green.log`, `/private/tmp/semantic-bulk-integration.log`). The focused V1 retention/worker/adapter suite passes **85 tests** in `/private/tmp/semantic-bulk-final-units.log`.

The normal 2,000-object rerun in `/private/tmp/semantic-bulk-capacity-green.log` used twenty 100-key S3 calls and deleted 1,000 per capped pass in **955 ms and 826 ms** with 178 competing reads and 178 writes. The normal minute cadence still has an absolute **1,000/minute** ceiling; this optimization alone does not resolve the paid-capacity burst or retention-change failure.

The optional `make semantic-retention-capacity-stress-check` keeps the production scheduler untouched while letting the disposable fixture exercise a larger per-run batch bound. An initial 20,000-object run used 200 bulk calls and finished in **24,859 ms** during 2,418 competing reads and 2,418 writes (`/private/tmp/semantic-bulk-capacity-stress.log`). A 22,000-object run used 220 bulk calls and finished in **29,719 ms** during 2,914 reads and 2,938 writes (`/private/tmp/semantic-bulk-capacity-stress-22k.log`). Both had zero reported S3 failures and verified the first and last objects absent. The latter exceeds the illustrative 20,452/minute paid Team retention-change lower bound **only in a small, zero-added-latency LocalStack fixture with a diagnostic 220-batch override**; it has about 281 ms before the existing 30-second start deadline and is not a safe configured throughput. The fixture contains 22,000 rows, not millions, and its competing updates touch separate job rows rather than production ingestion/projection hot paths. Hosted S3 latency, partial failures at load, receipt pruning, per-source fairness, worker contention and repeated sustained runs remain unqualified. No production schedule bound was raised.

A diagnostic `make semantic-retention-selection-stress-check` inserted one million synthetic receipt/index rows without one million S3 objects, then reduced one project's raw retention from 30 to one day. The original candidate selection cost about **170 ms** per bounded query; twenty repeated selections took **3,513 ms** in `/private/tmp/semantic-selection-hybrid.log`. A naive always-indexed split made the no-due case take **4,088 ms**, so it was not retained. The current hybrid uses the original broad first query and, only after it finds due work, advances a strict `(accepted_at,project_id,event_id)` cursor through separate indexed active/deleting branches. In the same million-row fixture, twenty cursor selections took **197 ms total**, with **1 ms median**; a no-due first query took **180 ms**. Real PostgreSQL/LocalStack tests cover equal acceptance timestamps and partial bulk failure. This is a one-project snapshot and does not prove many-project fairness, plan stability under concurrent writes, or end-to-end throughput at one million rows.

After cursor wiring, the normal ten-batch run still deleted 1,000 per pass (1,055 ms and 1,216 ms) in `/private/tmp/semantic-keyset-capacity-normal.log`. The diagnostic 22,000-object, 220-batch run improved to **23,610 ms** in `/private/tmp/semantic-keyset-capacity-stress.log`. An illustrative additional **50 ms per S3 bulk call** in `/private/tmp/semantic-keyset-capacity-latency.log` reached the 30-second deadline after **19,300 deletions**, leaving **2,700** due; this is below the 20,452/minute lower bound for the modeled paid Team retention-change backlog plus steady expiry. The 50-ms increment is a test assumption, not a measurement of hosted S3. The result demonstrates that the current per-object database work and sequential S3 batches still lack safe headroom even if the scheduler limit were raised. The production scheduler remains ten batches per minute.

## Activation gate

Before enabling V2 delivery or SDK capture, qualify a cleanup design against the purchased-unit maximum, allowed live bursts and retention-setting reductions while preserving the 24-hour lag rule. A candidate must demonstrate sustained deletion, backlog drain, per-project fairness, S3 partial-failure retry, bounded worker/DB load, receipt pruning and operator-visible progress at representative row/object counts and hosted-like latency. Increase throughput through measured bounded batches or multiple safely fenced owners, and bound aggregate arrival without changing installed V1 behavior; do not infer production capacity from this LocalStack result. Keep the existing V1 retention schedule independent.

A twelfth candidate migration now stores one payload-free loss count per project/UTC occurrence day. When expiry terminates an unfinished V2 job, the active-to-deleting receipt transition and the aggregate increment commit together; a marker failure rolls the transition back, and S3 retry does not count again. The marker survives transport receipt pruning and follows aggregate retention. Nineteen real PostgreSQL/LocalStack raw-retention cases and 54 focused migration/V1-retention units pass. This provides durable incomplete-evidence input but adds a database write to the loss path. Report processors still need to consume it with pending-work/source coverage; loss-day existence alone cannot establish an exact or complete report.

The disposable 22,000-object pending-job diagnostic now measures that additional write path. With the production scheduler still untouched and a **test-only 220-batch override**, it deleted all **22,000** objects, terminally marked 22,000 jobs and recorded exactly 22,000 loss markers in **24,640 ms** during 2,151 competing reads and 2,442 writes (`/private/tmp/semantic-loss-stress-22k.log`). Adding an illustrative **50 ms per S3 bulk call** made the 30-second deadline stop after **17,000** deletes/loss markers, leaving **5,000** due (`/private/tmp/semantic-loss-latency-22k.log`). The latter is below the **20,452/minute** illustrative paid Team backlog-plus-steady lower bound even before partial failures or hosted latency. Both one-off tests passed; neither measures sustained throughput, realistic hosted S3 latency, multi-project fairness, receipt-pruning load or actual report effects. The production minute job still has ten batches, at most **1,000 objects/minute**.

Project deletion is a separate erasure path. A focused Red test in `/private/tmp/semantic-s3-partial-red.log` showed that the shared prefix adapter ignored `DeleteObjects` per-key errors. It now throws `s3_object_deletion_incomplete`, with 31 focused adapter tests passing. The existing API project/account deletion callers still discard this failure after removing database rows, but a candidate eleventh forward migration now records an independent erasure task in the same DELETE transaction. The worker leases one 100-key S3 page at a time across all project prefixes, retries partial failures, verifies again after two hours and checks daily for late writes through 90 days. Four isolated PostgreSQL/LocalStack erasure cases and four existing project-deletion cases pass (`/private/tmp/semantic-erasure-worker-integration-3.log`, `/private/tmp/semantic-project-deletion-integration-3.log`). They establish retry ownership and actual absence for small fixtures, including the semantic prefix; they do not qualify the 24-hour individual-erasure lag, many-project worker capacity, 90-day task/list-request cost or hosted S3 behavior. The per-receipt expiry retry cannot recover objects after their project/receipt rows are gone; the project journal owns that separate path.

`make project-erasure-capacity-check` exercised 100 due deleted-project tasks against actual empty LocalStack prefixes in a disposable PostgreSQL/LocalStack project. The first scan required 900 leased worker claims and 800 S3 listings in **1,370 ms**; the forced verification scan required the same 900 claims and 800 listings in **1,328 ms** (`/private/tmp/project-erasure-capacity-1.log`). This is a small local idle-worker measurement with no objects, competing jobs or hosted network latency. The current daily verification design implies **8 listings per retained deleted-project task per day**, roughly **720 baseline listings per project across 90 days** in addition to the initial scan, before pagination/retries. This request and task-retention cost must be budgeted against real deletion volume; these timings do not establish an erasure SLA or justify activation.

`make project-erasure-capacity-latency-check` adds an illustrative **50 ms before each S3 LIST** in the same disposable LocalStack test and scans 20 empty deleted-project tasks twice. The scans made 180 leased claims and 160 listings each, taking **9,439 ms** and **9,298 ms** (`/private/tmp/project-erasure-capacity-latency-1.log`); all seven integration cases pass. At that synthetic latency, a single continuously dedicated worker would scan only about **183,000–186,000 empty tasks/day** before any real S3/DB contention, pages with objects, retries, restarts or other worker jobs. If every task remains for 90 days and needs a daily scan, that idealized rate sustains only roughly **2,000 new deletions/day** in steady state. This is a diagnostic upper-bound extrapolation, not hosted S3 latency or a deployment capacity claim. Worker ownership, scaled competing load and a measured individual 24-hour erasure SLA remain open.

[AWS S3 pricing](https://aws.amazon.com/s3/pricing/) says LIST requests are charged at the S3 Standard PUT/COPY/POST request rate and DELETE requests are free. Using the **illustrative** S3 Standard $0.005 per 1,000 LIST requests in this [recent AWS worked example](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/storage/run-spark-31-faster-and-optimize-compute-costs-with-amazon-s3-express-one-zone-on-amazon-emr/), the 720 daily-baseline listings imply about **$0.0036 per deleted project**, **$360 per 100,000 deleted projects** or **$3,600 per million**, before initial/verification scans, extra pages, retries, task-row/worker compute and regional price differences. The hosted template names `eu-central-1`; this illustrative rate is **not** a verified Frankfurt bill rate or a production-volume forecast. The 90-day policy's cost and worker capacity remain open.
